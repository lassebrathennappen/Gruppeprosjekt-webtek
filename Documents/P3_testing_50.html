1. Administrative details

Imaginary client name: Fedon (he changed his legal name to be more Greek)

Website name: OPA!

Contact person: Fedon, owner of OPA!

OPA! is a Greek restaurant in Trondheim city centre (Munkegata 1) serving traditional Greek food with a modern twist. The website lets visitors read about the restaurant, browse the food and drinks menu, find events and special offers, and reserve a table.

2. Testing document

Our main method was live, observed usability testing. Each tester was given seven tasks, read aloud one at a time so that later tasks did not give away earlier answers. We did not explain how to navigate the site, and we did not help during the tasks. For each task we wrote down the time spent, the path the tester took in the navigation, any mistakes, and direct quotes. Testers also rated each task from 1 (very easy) to 5 (very difficult).

Tasks

  1. Find the opening hours.
  2. Find out if there are any drinks that tempt you.
  3. Find out where the restaurant is located.
  4. Book a table.
  5. Read the history of the restaurant.
  6. Find out if the restaurant has any special offers or events.
  7. Find the menu for the event.

Why we did not use a Google Form

The assignment states:

“Students often create a Google Form and send it to as many people as possible to get feedback. While this allows for gathering plenty of feedback, it is not a substitute for the ‘real observation’ of someone using your website. Thus, either during the lab session or in some other College setting, try to get a small number of users to do live testing of your website, while you are present to observe them. This will give you invaluable insight into how people are navigating and interacting with your website.”

It also asks for:

“A brief summary of the questionnaire you send out to testers, as well as the link to the Google Form you used. Don’t forget to ask your client to test the website.”

We understood this to mean that live testing is more valuable than a Google Form. Since the assignment asks us to try to get a small number of users to test live, we chose to do many live tests instead. We see a Google Form as a supplement for groups who cannot find enough people to test live. Every tester was observed in person using the tasks above, and we recorded the results in a shared observation sheet.

5. Feedback summary

All three testers completed all seven tasks without help (21 of 21 tasks, 100 %). Almost every task was rated 1, which suggests the overall structure and navigation work well.

Navigation

Most testers navigated directly to the correct page from the main menu. The About us page was generally found within 1–10 seconds, while the Events page took 5–16 seconds to locate. Most testers expected practical information under About us, rather than personal stories. The menu functioned as intended on smaller screens. S ome testers suggested that the navigation bar should remain sticky while scrolling, as they otherwise had to scroll all the way back to the top to access other pages.

Opening hours were the information that required the most searching. Several testers initially looked under About us or on the homepage, while others expected to find them under Contact. Most eventually located the opening hours in the footer, but the different expectations suggest that this information was not placed where users naturally expected to find it.

“Contact is nice too. I think the opening hours should be there.” – T3 (translated)

"The website is intuitive." - T10 (translated)

"I remember finding this information in the footer earlier." - T4 (translated)

"I like that I'm easily able to edit previous choices in the booking form" - T3 (translated)

Content

The location was easy to find, as the address is displayed on the homepage, in the footer and on the Contact page. This was also positively noted by two testers. The Events page received positive feedback for its clear heading. Several testers also commented that the amount of content and text on the website was good, with enough information without feeling overwhelming.

However, the grill menu was not immediately recognised as a special offer or deal. Some testers were unsure whether it was a regular menu or a discounted offer available on certain days. It was therefore suggested to make this clearer by highlighting that the grill menu is a special offer with lower prices on specific days. There was also some confusion because the same grill menu appeared under both Menu and Events. Clearer naming, such as “Special grill menu”, and a more visible link or button connecting the two pages could help users understand the relationship between them.

Usability

Booking a table took the longest on all devices (39 s to 1 min 32 s), yet all testers rated it 1 or 2 (easy). The bookin process received positive feedback, particularly for the ability to review and edit selections and navigate between text fields using the arrow keys.

“I love that I can jump between text fields with the arrow keys.” – T3 (translated)

“I like that I can see what I have chosen and edit it.” – T1 (translated)

The step-by-step process also worked well when making changes. Previously selected information, such as the date, was retained when navigating back and forth between steps. This made it easier to adjust the number of guests without having to re-enter the date, which was appreciated by testers.

“I like that the page saves my information if I have to change something in the booking form” – T5 (translated)

Design and responsiveness

The website layout generally worked well across devices, but some differences were noticed between desktop, tablet and mobile. The navigation buttons appeared quite small on mobile and were easier to use on a computer. On the iPad, the images on the Events page appeared distorted. The menu options on the Menu page adapted well to the different screen sizes. Some inconsistensies were also found in the hover effect of the Reservations button. The button looked different depending on whether it was accessed from the navigation bar or the homepage. These hover effects should be consistent to make it clearer to users when they are hovering over an interactive element. In the footer, the opening hours were also positioned very close to the logo. Apart from these issues, the overall layout and responsiveness were considered good.

Accessibility

T3 is visually impaired and had no trouble with the contrast or the font size on the website.

6. Our response

We have not changed the website after testing. Below is what we would do to address the feedback.

Show the opening hours where people look for them

Most of the testers expected the opening hours somewhere other than the footer. We would add them to the About Us and Contact pages and show them near the address on the home page. Most testers assumed the About Us page was more "practical info" as well as the story behind the restaurant, therefore, we would treat it as such and provide all practical info there as well. The footer can keep them as well, since it is consistent across pages.

Make the event menu clearer

We would rename the grill menu on the Menu page to “Special grill menu” and add a short note that on selected days we have an event deal for this menu, with a link to the events page. On the events page, we would add a “See the menu” link that jumps to the menu section using an anchor, plus a clear heading above the menu to make it even clearer that this is what we offer for the event.

Improve the tablet layout

These issues came up when the iPad tester was asked to scroll around the website after the test, in both landscape and portrait orientation. For narrow screens, we would adjust the events image with a media query and give the menu more width, as it was unnecessarily narrow in our last version. In the footer we would make the logo smaller and add more spacing between the logo and the opening hours. We would make the menu into a hamburger menu and sticky to the top.

Improve the Phone layout

For the phone, we would make the menu into a hamburger menu and sticky to the top, as on the iPad.

7. Self-reflection

From the group project we learned how to build a website from scratch. We learned how to use GitHub and work together as a team, and how a website is structured using HTML, CSS and JavaScript. Most of what we learned came from trying and testing the code ourselves, and we quickly found out that even small mistakes, like a missing bracket or a wrong file path, can cause big problems. We also learned how to put repeating code, such as the navbar, in JavaScript, so we don’t have to repeat it in every file. This made the site easier to maintain, since a change only has to be made in one place. The user testing taught us something we could not have learned on our own. As developers we knew exactly where everything was, so the site felt obvious to us. Watching real people use it showed us otherwise: two of three testers looked for the opening hours in places where they were not, and one tester was confused by the grill menu appearing on two different pages. These are small details, but they show how easy it is to design for yourself instead of for the user. Testing on different devices also showed us layout problems on the iPad in portrait mode that we had not noticed while working on our laptops. Even though the project was fun, it took a lot of time, and we sometimes felt we would rather have used that time on other parts of the course. We also wished the assignments had a clearer structure and were more explicit about what was expected. There was a lot of text, and it was sometimes difficult to figure out what we were supposed to deliver. If we were to do the project again, we would test the website with real users earlier, while it was still easy to make changes, instead of only at the end. We would also agree on a shared structure for files and code from the start, so that it would be easier to combine everyone’s work.
